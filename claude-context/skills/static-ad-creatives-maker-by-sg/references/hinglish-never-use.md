# Hinglish and Hindi ad lines: words and combinations to screen out (native-reader screen, Daigo round 4, 2026-10-10)

Why this file exists: Soumil ruled out "Phaado. Daalo. Hilao." as very controversial after it had gone into five finished creatives; the verbs tear, put in, shake in that order read as a crude innuendo in Hindi slang, and nobody had been screening for it. A separate native-reader agent now screens every line (SKILL.md Stage 4a, policy-checklist row 13). This is the list that reader produced on 127 lines; extend it every round. It is a screen, not a legal opinion; LOW flags are fine to ship with a note, MED and HIGH are rewritten.

Method (reusable): `references/templates/sensitivity_template.md` plus `scripts/make_copy_inputs.py` build the input from a pool file; the reader returns one table (PASS or FLAG, severity, the unintended reading, a safe rewrite), this never-use list, local-slang notes and scene-note risks. Include known-bad calibration lines in the input to prove the reader catches them (it did: HIGH on all three). Budget 30 to 40 minutes for about 130 lines.

## Never use (HIGH or MED in the screen)
1. phaado, phaad, phaadna together with daalo or hilao, in any order or part. The English mirror "Tear. Add water. Shake." is LOW but still reads as a translation; prefer "Sachet. Water. Shake." or "Open. Add water. Shake."
2. hilao, hila, hilana, hila ke in any use. Safe: "shake karo", "mix karo".
3. daalo, daal do, daal diya, andar as the verb for adding anything. Safe: "milao", "lo".
4. paani with daalo, nikalo, chhodo, girao or bahao (bodily-fluid slang).
5. Three bare verbs in a row with no object (Kholo. Milao. Piyo. / Tear. Shake. Sip.): put a noun after each verb.
6. The ask-and-hand-over script: "Bhai, ___ hai? Haan. Bag mein."; "___ chahiye? Bag mein hai."; "Got ___? Check the bag."; whispering; a packet passed out of a bag. "Chaas" is one syllable from "charas": keep chaas away from every such script. Also: maal, stash, supply, dose, fix, hit, puff, pudiya, light up, high, baked, stoned.
7. Stamina and condom-pack framing: "won't last", "kam padenge", "jaldi khatam", "finish", "size", "fits", "slim", "pocket rocket"; any "20 ka pack" next to "kam padenge". Small foil packets plus a count (10, 20) already invite this reading, so keep copy and picture away from it.
8. Touch and threat: bare "touch it", "haath lagana" (also means hit or grope), "hands on", "ek bhi gaya toh...", "dekh lunga", "warna", "hisaab chukta karna". "Hands off." in English is fine.
9. Alcohol coding: clink, cheers, toast, shot, peg, "pack lagao" (also: "pack hai" in some circles), bottoms up, chug, on the rocks, chaser, hangover, buzz; a bottle in a car boot; anyone holding a drink at the wheel; two tumblers clinking.
10. Political: "chai pe charcha", "chaiwala", "main bhi ...", "achhe din", any party slogan, "Har ghar ..." formats; any jab at the Railways during an accident or delay news cycle.
11. "& chill"; "dikki" for the car boot (use "boot" or "gaadi"); "dukaan khuli hai"; item, garam, tight, hard, wet, dip, stuff, load, slurp, swallow, squeeze, release, "tear it open", "pour it in".
12. Wrong-word traps: "rate" for price (use "daam"); "easy" said of a feminine noun like chaas (use "aasaan"); "set karna" or "setting" for curd (also dating slang; use "jamana" or anchor with "curd"); "khud" next to "bada ho gaya".
13. "pocket" or "jeb" next to a sachet held in a hand or tucked in a hoodie pocket; "pack" with bag, pocket or boot.
14. Sound-alikes of crude words: chhodo and chhod do, load and lode, gand and gandi, any "chut-" opening, jhaat, "lamba" in any size context.
15. The numbers 69, 420, 786, 143 and 108 anywhere: prices, room numbers, counters, codes.
16. Threat, theft and reader-insult tone: "Stop stealing", chor, "utha li", "Cute" (talks down), "noob"; anything that casts the reader as a thief or a fool. "Two [plural]. One [singular]." echoes a shock meme.
17. Festival, fasting, diet and community claims unless verified: Janmashtami, Dahi Handi, Holi with bhaang or thandai, Navratri, vrat, ekadashi, sattvik, Jain, halal; "chaas at night" (folk belief against dinner-time curd).
17b. Frames that echo habit or drink idioms (second screen): "Another X. Another [habit]" (coping-drink joke; a sachet also reads as pan masala or gutka, use "Another chaas break"); "ek ___ toh banta hai" (echoes "ek peg toh banta hai" and "ek sutta toh banta hai"; daytime scenes only, never "aur ek?"); an unlabelled clear bottle beside a tumbler (passes for spirits: show a water label or use the shaker); capitals "POOCHNA" (opens with POO: write "PUCH LENA").
18. Format: more than one exclamation mark per line, a stretched-letter run, long all-caps sentences risk Meta review delay (not safety). Allowed as ONE deliberate loud poster per round (Daigo 15, "SOOOO MUCH CHAAS!!!", the energy of his approved "SOOOOOOOO MUCH WATER!!!"); a stretched word that sounds like a rude one is worse (CHAAAAAS reads near "charas").

## Local slang that may not travel
PG (Delhi, Bengaluru, Pune, Hyderabad; Mumbai says paying guest), mess (hostel dining hall; in Mumbai and Pune a home-style tiffin eatery), jhanjhat (Hindi belt; East jhamela, Mumbai locha, South tension), jeb (use pocket), dahi set karna, chaas itself (Maharashtra taak, Karnataka majjige, Andhra majjiga, Tamil Nadu and Kerala mor, Bengal ghol: South and East readers may need "buttermilk" in small print), boot versus dikki, Sleeper and CNF (rail jargon, opaque to people who only fly), Mummy (Aai, Ma, Amma elsewhere). Chaas is feminine: "ye meri hai", not "mera".

## Picture risks that make a safe line risky
- Hands tearing a sachet beside a verb headline: never a close-up of ripping; show a notched corner or the powder already in the shaker.
- White powder or white liquid pouring or splashing (reads as gym or drug powder, or a crude joke): show a pale yellow-green mix with spice flecks, drinks poured into a steel tumbler, no splashes, no dark mirror surface, no rolled notes or cards.
- Sachets in a hoodie pocket, a pile of loose packets, a sachet handed out of a bag: stash reading. Print the brand mark clearly on every sachet, stack neatly, put sachets on the table or in an open hand.
- A railway ticket: fictional, no IRCTC or Railways logo, no real PNR or train number.
- Clinking tumblers, a bottle in a car boot with a highway behind: keep tumblers apart or one raised; the bottle is a clear water bottle, the car parked.
- A person sitting on a window sill or ledge; words like akela or udaas near it.
- Handwriting that looks angry on a threat-style note; appliance brands or company names on lanyards.
