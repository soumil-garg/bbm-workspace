## 2. WORDS AND COMBINATIONS TO NEVER USE IN DAIGO ADS

Items 1 to 10 matter most. The rest are narrower traps found while screening.

1. phaado, phaad, phaadna together with daalo or hilao, in any order, with or without objects, in Hindi or Hinglish. Also do not run the English mirror "Tear. Add water. Shake." as the main instruction (use "Open. Add water. Shake.").
2. hilao, hila, hilana, hila ke, in any use. Safe: "shake karo", "mix karo".
3. daalo, daal do, daal diya, andar, as the verb for adding anything. Safe: "milao", "lo".
4. paani with daalo, nikalo, nikal, chhodo, girao or bahao. In those pairings "paani" carries a bodily-fluid slang sense.
5. Three bare verbs in a row with no object (Kholo. Milao. Piyo. / Tear. Shake. Sip.). Put a noun after each verb.
6. The ask-and-hand-over script: "Bhai, ___ hai? Haan. Bag mein."; "___ chahiye? Bag mein hai."; "Got ___? Check the bag."; whispering; a packet passed out of a bag. "Chaas" is one syllable from "charas", so keep chaas away from every such script. Also: maal, stash, supply, dose, fix, hit, puff, pudiya, light up, high, baked, stoned.
7. Stamina and condom-pack framing: "won't last", "kam padenge", "jaldi khatam", "finish", "size", "fits", "slim", "pocket rocket", and any "20 ka pack" next to "kam padenge".
8. Touch and threat: bare "touch it", "haath lagana", "hands on", "ek bhi gaya toh...", "dekh lunga", "warna", "hisaab chukta karna".
9. Alcohol coding: clink, cheers, toast, shot, peg, "pack lagao", bottoms up, chug, on the rocks, chaser, hangover, buzz. Also a bottle in a car boot, or anyone holding a drink at the wheel.
10. Political: "chai pe charcha", "chaiwala", "main bhi ...", "achhe din", any party slogan, "Har ghar ..." formats, and any jab at the Railways during an accident or delay news cycle.
11. "& chill" (Chaas and chill); "dikki" or "dickey" for the car boot (use "boot" or "gaadi"); "dukaan khuli hai" or "shutter" (an open-fly joke); item, garam, tight, hard, wet, dip, stuff, load, slurp, swallow, squeeze, release, "tear it open", "pour it in".
12. Wrong-word traps: "rate" for price (use "daam"); "easy" said of chaas (use "aasaan"); "set karna" or "setting" for curd (use "jamana"); "khud" and "bada ho gaya" in one line.
13. "pocket" or "jeb" next to a sachet held in a hand or tucked in a hoodie pocket; "pack" with bag, pocket or boot ("pack hai", "packing").
14. Sound-alikes of crude words: chhodo and chhod do (one letter from a crude verb), load and lode (Hinglish "lode lag gaye"), gand and gandi, any "chut-" opening, jhaat, and "lamba" in any size context.
15. The numbers 69, 420, 786, 143 and 108 anywhere: prices, room numbers, counters, codes.
16. Threat, theft and reader-insult tone: "Stop stealing", chor, "utha li", "Cute", "noob", anything that casts the reader as a thief or a fool.
17. Festival, fasting, diet and community claims unless verified: Janmashtami, Dahi Handi, Holi with bhaang or thandai, Navratri, vrat, ekadashi, sattvik, Jain, halal, and "chaas at night". None are in the current set; keep it so.
18. Format: more than one exclamation mark per line, stretched-letter runs beyond one per ad, long all-caps sentences. The approved water shout is the founder's call; the risk is review delay under Meta's punctuation and bad-grammar standard, not safety.

## 3. Local slang that may feel alien outside one city

- "PG" (5.1): everyday in Delhi NCR, Bengaluru, Pune, Hyderabad. Mumbai says "paying guest" or "sharing flat"; Kolkata and Chennai say "hostel" or "mess".
- "mess": in hostels it means the dining hall; in Mumbai and Pune it means a home-style tiffin eatery. Lines 5.9 and 18.5 also use "mess" for chaos inside the same set, so the two senses collide.
- "jhanjhat" (18.1): Hindi belt. East says "jhamela", Mumbai "locha", the South "tension" or "hassle". Safer: "tension nahi".
- "jeb" (16.4, 16.5): Hindi and Urdu belt. Hinglish speakers elsewhere say "pocket".
- "dahi set karna" (18.2): Delhi and Mumbai Hinglish. The Hindi belt says "dahi jamana"; the South says "curd" or "thayir".
- "chaas" itself: Gujarat, Rajasthan, Delhi, UP and Haryana say chaas or chhachh. Maharashtra says "taak", Karnataka "majjige", Andhra and Telangana "majjiga", Tamil Nadu and Kerala "mor" or "moru", Bengal "ghol". South and East readers may need the word "buttermilk" in small print.
- "boot" for a car trunk (8.2, 8.7): Indian usage is "dikki", which has an unsafe English echo. "Boot" is understood mainly by car buyers. "Gaadi mein" is safer.
- "Sleeper", "CNF", "Waitlisted" (1.x): rail jargon, clear to regular train travellers; "CNF" is opaque to people who only fly.
- "Mummy" (10.x): Delhi, Punjab, Gujarat, Mumbai. Maharashtra says "Aai", Bengal "Ma", the South "Amma". The chat skit lands best in the North.
- Grammar, not slang: "chaas" is feminine in Hindi. 6.3 "ye mera hai" works only if "ye" is the glass or sachet; if it means the chaas it should be "ye meri hai".
- Fine everywhere in Hindi-reading India: mazaak, hisaab, dhoyega, chhutti, khatam, shuru, sorted.

## 4. Scene-note risks that make an otherwise safe line risky

- K picture (hands tearing a sachet over a shaker): amplifies K.1 to K.3 and the English mirror (2.5, S.1, S.2). Never show a close-up of hands ripping a sachet beside any verb headline. Show a notched corner, or the powder already in the shaker.
- 1 (railway ticket under the pouch): use a fictional ticket, with no Indian Railways or IRCTC logo and no real PNR, train number or name. With the picture, 1.3 and 1.10 read as a jab at the Railways.
- 2 (powder pouring into a shaker bottle): a white-powder-in-a-bottle look reads as gym or drug powder. Show a pale yellow-green mix with visible spice flecks on a light kitchen surface, no dark mirror surface, no rolled notes or cards.
- 4 (two sachets in a hoodie pocket): turns 4.8 into a packet-in-pocket reading. Put the sachets on the table or in the hand with the print visible.
- 5 (dirty mixer jar full of foam): show soap suds as clear bubbles and food residue, not an overflowing white liquid, and no appliance brand or logo on the jar (brand disparagement). The picture also keeps "mixer" in the kitchen; as text alone "mixer" can read as the soda that goes with a drink, so do not run 5.x text-only.
- 6 (masking-tape note): with 6.1 and 6.2 the note reads as a threat if the handwriting looks angry. Keep it playful.
- 7 (two hands clink two tumblers): clinking is the "cheers" pose and sets up an alcohol toast. Keep the tumblers apart or one raised. No readable company name on the lanyard.
- 8 (bottle beside a bag in a car boot, highway behind): the liquor-in-the-car picture. The bottle must be a clearly labelled water bottle or the shaker, the car parked, nobody at the wheel holding a tumbler.
- 9 (pile of loose sachets, two hands on one sachet): a heap of loose foil packets reads as a stash, and the two hands feed the 9.6 meme echo. Print the Daigo mark clearly on every sachet and stack them neatly.
- 10 (phone chat): staged skit is fine. No real names and no real contact photos.
- 11 (window sill at dusk): still life only. No person sitting on a sill or ledge (self-harm reading). Do not add "akela", "udaas" or "alone".
- 12 (dull mess thali against a bright tumbler): reads as a dig at hostel food. Fine, but name no hostel or college, and keep the thali plainly vegetarian.
- 13 (backpack spills sachets): same stash look as 9. Show printed sachets and the tumbler in front.
- 15 (friend pulls a sachet out of a bag): the handover picture; it is what turns 15.3 and 15.4 from harmless to risky. Re-shoot with the sachet standing on the table beside the pouch, both friends open and visible, one holding a tumbler.
- 16 (slim sachet held like a pen): reads as a wrapper held between fingers and feeds the size joke. Hold it by the corner with the print facing the camera and the pouch behind it, or show a hand holding a tumbler with the sachet beside it.
- 17 (chaas splash): see 17.1. No white splash, no slow-motion squirt shapes, no cream colour on a dark background.
- 3, 14, 18: nothing found in the picture notes.
- Whole set: pale or white liquid falling or splashing (2, 5, 17) should be shot as a drink poured into a steel tumbler, not as splashes.
